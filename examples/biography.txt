Marie Curie was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, when Poland was under Russian rule. She left for Paris in 1891 to study at the Sorbonne, where she met the physicist Pierre Curie. They married in 1895 and began their collaborative work on radioactivity, a term Marie herself coined.

Together, Pierre and Marie Curie discovered polonium, which Marie named after her native country, and radium. In 1903, they shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Henri Becquerel. After Pierre's death in 1906, Marie took over his teaching position at the Sorbonne, becoming the first woman to hold a professorship there. She won a second Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1911.

During the First World War, Marie organized mobile radiography units for the French Army on the front lines. She later founded the Radium Institute in Paris and the Radium Institute in Warsaw, both of which became major centers of medical and scientific research. Her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie, working alongside her husband Frédéric Joliot, would go on to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1935.
